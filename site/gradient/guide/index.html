How to make a block gradient

A guide to smooth color gradients in Minecraft Java 26.3: which blocks blend well, what ruins a gradient, and ready-made gradients with every block listed. Want your own colors? Use the block gradient generator.

The short version

  1. Pick two end colors and decide whether it is a wall or a floor. Many blocks look different from the top than from the side, so match the face people will see.
  2. Choose 6 to 10 blocks that step evenly from one color to the other. Equal steps in brightness matter more than an exact hue.
  3. Stick to calm textures. Concrete, wool, terracotta and stripped logs blend well. Busy blocks like bookshelves, ores and glazed terracotta break the flow.
  4. Avoid repeats unless the row is long. Two neighbors with nearly the same color look like a flat stripe.
  5. Blend the seams. Instead of hard bands, scatter a few blocks of each color into its neighbor's band (dithering). It hides the jumps between steps.
  6. Check it in game from the distance people will see it. Up close every gradient looks noisy; from 20 blocks away it should read as one smooth fade.

Which blocks are best for gradients?

We measured the average color and the texture noise of every full solid block in Java 26.3: 460 faces in all, counting a log's bark and its cut end as separate options.

  • Concrete is the smoothest block in the game. All 16 colors have almost no texture, so they make the cleanest gradients, but they can look flat in large areas.
  • Wool and concrete powder add a soft texture in the same 16 colors. Concrete powder falls like sand, so use it in floors, not walls.
  • Terracotta gives muted, earthy versions of the 16 colors and fills the gaps between bright concrete shades.
  • Stripped logs and planks cover browns, tans and pinks (cherry, pale oak) that the dyed blocks miss.
  • Stone types (andesite, diorite, tuff, deepslate, basalt, blackstone) are the go-to for grays.
  • Avoid ores, bookshelves, glazed terracotta and anything with a strong pattern. They draw the eye to one block instead of the whole fade.

Why gradients look bumpy

Most bad gradients come from picking each block as the closest match to its target color, one at a time. The closest match can be a little too light, the next a little too dark, and the row zigzags. Our generator scores the whole row at once, so every block also has to fit its neighbors.

The other cause is mixing colors in RGB, which makes midpoints look muddy and dark. We blend in OKLab, a color space where equal steps look equal to the eye.
